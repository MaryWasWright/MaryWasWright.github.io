Keep the Dewclaws: Why a Great Pyrenees' Double Dewclaws Stay

Those extra toes on each hind leg belong there. Here's what they are, why they stay, and how to care for them.

← All Field Notes

Turn a Great Pyrenees' back foot over in your hand and you'll find something most dogs don't have: two extra toes on the inside of each hind leg, set above the pad. Those are the double dewclaws, and in this breed they belong there. Our rule is simple: hind double dewclaws stay unless injured.

The breed standard asks for them

The official AKC standard for the Great Pyrenees says double dewclaws “are located on each rear leg,” and it lists their absence as a fault. Each front leg carries a single dewclaw. On a Pyr, those extra toes are a mark of the breed, not a flaw to fix.

What they're thought to do

The honest answer is that the science on rear dewclaws is thin. No large study has measured how much a Pyr relies on them. What Pyr people describe is help with footing. Breed clubs say the dewclaws are thought to have a “snow-shoe” effect: the wide, extra-toed hind foot may spread the dog's weight and steady it on snow, scree, and broken rock. Treat that as a long-held, observed belief rather than a proven fact.

Why routine removal is discouraged

A dewclaw is a toe, not just a nail. Removing one means amputating a digit, with its bone, nerves, and blood supply. Breeders once clipped them off newborn puppies out of habit, to prevent injuries that might never happen or to tidy a leg's look. Veterinary thinking has moved away from that. The Australian Veterinary Association, for example, calls routine dewclaw removal unacceptable without a valid medical reason. It says preventive removal of hind dewclaws should happen only when there's a real risk of injury, judged case by case, never as routine.

A Pyr has one more reason to keep them: removal takes away something the breed standard calls for. Done for looks or convenience, it costs the dog and gives nothing back.

When a vet may need to step in

None of this makes a dewclaw untouchable. A badly torn dewclaw, a deep infection, a tumor, or a toe that keeps getting hurt can all be good reasons to treat it, and sometimes to remove it. That's a medical decision for your veterinarian, made with proper pain relief. Keeping dewclaws is the default. It doesn't mean ignoring a problem.

Caring for those extra toes

  • Trim them. Dewclaws sit off the ground, so they don't wear down. Part the fluff to find them. The Great Pyrenees Club of America warns they can grow in a circle and dig into the pad. Trim them every time you do the other nails.
  • Check for snags. After a romp through brush or rocks, look for a cracked or bent nail, limping, licking, or blood.
  • Start young. Handle puppy feet often and pair it with treats, so nail trims stay calm when your dog weighs 100 pounds.

A torn or bleeding dewclaw hurts and can get infected, so call your vet instead of waiting it out.

Two extra toes, two good reasons: they're part of the breed, and they're part of the dog. Keep them trimmed, keep an eye on them, and let them be.
