Keep the Coat: Why We Don't Shave a Great Pyrenees for Texas Summer

That big white coat is insulation and sunshade. Here's why shaving backfires, and what actually helps.

← All Field Notes

When a Texas July settles in and the thermometer pushes past a hundred, a Great Pyrenees can look like the most overdressed animal in the county. It's natural to wonder whether a summer shave would be a kindness. Veterinarians and breed experts give the same answer: no. That big white coat is doing more work in the heat than it seems.

A coat built for both seasons

A Pyr wears a double coat: a soft, woolly undercoat close to the skin, with longer, coarse guard hairs over the top. Together they hold a layer of still air, and still air carries heat poorly. In winter that layer keeps warmth in. In warm weather the dog sheds much of its undercoat, and what remains helps keep outside heat away from the skin. The mostly white outer coat also reflects sunlight and shades the skin beneath it, a little like a wide-brimmed hat.

Shaving takes away that insulation and gives the dog no better way to cool down. A veterinarian at Texas A&M notes that shaved double-coated dogs can actually become less tolerant of high temperatures.

What shaving can cost

A close shave leaves bare skin open to the sun, and sunburn is a real risk. It can also change the coat itself. The undercoat tends to grow back first and can crowd the slower guard hairs, so a shaved double coat may return uneven, patchy, or with a different texture.

Veterinary dermatologists also describe post-clipping alopecia, where hair is slow to return after a close clip. It shows up more often in plush, thick-coated breeds. Most coats come back within three or four months, but some take a year or two.

There are honest exceptions. Severe matting, surgery, or a skin problem can call for clipping. That's a decision to make with your veterinarian or a groomer who knows double coats.

What actually helps in a Texas summer

  • Brush and rake. A weekly brushing, plus an undercoat rake when your dog is “blowing” coat, pulls out the loose fluff so air can move through what's left. A coat packed with dead undercoat can't do its job.
  • Shade, always. A good tree or shade structure can feel 10 to 15 degrees cooler than open sun.
  • Water, always. Keep fresh, cool water within reach all day.
  • Cool ground. Damp, shaded earth, a cooling mat, or a cool floor gives a Pyr somewhere to lie down and shed heat.
  • Move the work. Save walks and play for early morning or late evening, never midday, and keep in mind how hot the pavement gets.

Know the warning signs

Heavy panting, drooling, whining, seeking shade, and not wanting to play can be early signs that a dog is overheating. Bring your dog into air conditioning, offer water, and wet the coat with cool water. With a Pyr, make sure it soaks all the way down to the skin. A fan helps too.

If the heavy panting and drooling don't ease, or you see trouble breathing, vomiting, bloody diarrhea, weakness, confusion, seizures, or collapse, treat it as an emergency. Call your veterinarian and go right away. Heatstroke can damage organs, and it's life-threatening.

Our rule here is simple: don't shave the coat for Texas summer. Keep it brushed, keep your dog in the shade, and let that coat do the job it was built for.
