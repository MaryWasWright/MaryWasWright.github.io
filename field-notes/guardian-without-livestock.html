No Sheep Required: A Great Pyrenees Can Guard Your Family

A Pyr is a guardian, and guarding is the job. In a family home, the flock is you.

← All Field Notes

“Don't you need sheep for one of those?” Pyr owners hear it all the time. The short answer is no. A Great Pyrenees is a guardian dog, and guarding is the job. On a ranch, the flock is goats and sheep. In a family home, the flock is you: your people, your house, your yard. Raised and trained well, a Pyr takes that work just as seriously.

Guarding is instinct, not a trick

The Great Pyrenees Club of America describes Pyrs as territorial guard dogs “by instinct, not by training.” They were bred to think and act on their own, out with the flock while the shepherd slept. The club notes that working Pyrs often look after the farm family as well as the stock, and the AKC says today's Pyrs make exceptional guardians of home and loved ones. Livestock is one place to aim that instinct. A family is another.

At home, that looks like a big white dog lying where he can see the door, getting up to check a strange sound, and stepping between his people and anything unfamiliar. A well-raised Pyr is not an attack dog. He should accept the guests you welcome inside. He just won't welcome uninvited ones.

What a family Pyr needs

  • A real fence. Pyrs patrol, and without a boundary they'll keep widening their territory. The breed club recommends a fence at least six feet high with secure gates. It says invisible or underground fencing doesn't work for this breed.
  • A leash everywhere else. Outside the fence, keep a Pyr on leash. A Pyr with somewhere to go rarely comes when called.
  • Early socialization. Introduce a young Pyr to lots of people, places, and calm dogs, so the adult can tell a guest from a threat.
  • Patience with an independent thinker. Pyrs learn fast but get bored with drills. Short, varied, positive sessions work better than repetition.
  • A job and a routine. A daily walk along the fence line, a regular post on the porch, and predictable evenings give a guardian a sense of order.

About the night barking

Pyrs are naturally most watchful after dark, and barking is their first warning to anything out there. The breed club says it plainly: Pyrs are barkers, especially at night, and most that live in towns or suburbs should be kept indoors at night. That's usually the kindest answer for your dog and your neighbors. You can manage the barking. You can't train the guardian out of the dog.

Honest fit notes

  • Size. Adults stand 25 to 32 inches tall and often weigh 85 to 100 pounds or more. Supervise them around small children, and teach manners before the puppy outgrows you.
  • Shedding. Expect weekly brushing, much more when the coat blows, and white hair on everything. (And please don't shave it.)
  • Not a dog-park dog. Off-leash parks and open trails aren't safe for a breed that roams.
  • Space, not miles. Pyrs aren't marathon runners, and most are happy lounging and watching. Still, a yard to patrol suits them far better than an apartment.

No sheep required. Give a Pyr a secure fence, a family to watch over, and steady, patient raising, and he'll do the job he was bred for: keeping his flock safe.
